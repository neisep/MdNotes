How this review was done

Every production file under MdNotes/ was read in full rather than sampled, and three systematic sweeps were run across the whole tree — one over every catch block and error-swallowing construct, one over every path and text-matching expression, one tracing four realistic feature changes to every site they touch. Nothing here is called systemic on the strength of a single example; where a pattern is claimed to repeat, every occurrence is listed.

The test suite was run once, read-only, to establish a baseline: 467 passed, 2 failed, 3 skipped. The two failures are Linux-only and are themselves a finding (M8). Nothing in the repository was modified.

1Executive Summary

This is a well-maintained codebase. That is the honest headline and it should be read before the findings, because a list of twenty-eight numbered problems reads worse than the code actually is. Layering is strict, side effects are contained, state transitions are small and explicit, and the doc comments are the best thing here: they record why a decision was made, what was tried before, and what it cost — NoteContentCache carries measured timings, AppServices records the fourteen-site edit it eliminated, TreeNodeViewModel.Same explains why it is deliberately stricter than its neighbour. Most reviews of a 6 500-line app do not find that.

Against that baseline, the findings fall into four groups.

  • Two real defects. Autosave failures escape the project's own error convention and surface as crash-barrier messages (H1), and a corrupt settings.json is an unrecoverable startup crash before any UI exists to report it (H2). Both are small fixes in single files.
  • A recurring shape: a comment asserts a rule the code does not enforce. "The one place that decides" above a constant declared four times (M1); "the interval the other two timers use" above three unlinked literals (M13); "The one place that answers it" above one of three fallbacks (L11); a precondition documented in a caller and honoured by no other (M7). The comments are the codebase's greatest asset, which is exactly why the ones that have quietly become false matter.
  • Settings are the one genuine change-amplification hotspot. Adding a single toggle is eight mandatory edits, three of them in one file — and then a choice between four different, undocumented ways of making the setting take effect, one of which already leaves a second window showing stale values (CA1). By contrast, adding a service is one line, and adding an overlay is genuinely cheap. The project has solved this problem twice already and simply has not solved it here.
  • A long tail of small inconsistencies — four caches with four eviction policies, three filesystem error policies, one case-sensitive path comparison among nine insensitive ones — each harmless today, each a place where two readings of the same question can drift apart.

Nothing here calls for restructuring. The highest-value work is roughly a day: two guards, one try/catch, one constant, and making four comments true again.

2Maintainability Overview

Conventions, as actually practised. The house style is consistent enough that departures from it stand out: expression-bodied members, C# pattern matching (is { } x, switch expressions, list patterns), single statements without braces, ObservableProperty source generators for view-model state, and constructor-injected dependencies held in readonly fields. Nullable reference types are on project-wide (MdNotes.csproj:7) with only nine ! operators in the whole tree.

Error handling is a deliberate two-tier design, documented at DialogGuard.cs:5-18 and pinned by ErrorFunnelTests: failures a command can foresee go through Guard and reach the user in the words they were thrown with; anything that escapes hits the crash barrier at App.axaml.cs:38, which says the failure was unexpected. The second message is meant to be a signal that a guard is missing. That design is good, and it is the yardstick H1 is measured against — the finding is not that the convention is wrong but that two call sites escape it.

State management is clean. One VaultService per process behind a revision-stamped snapshot; view models read it rather than caching it, with a documented reason (VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:75-79). Background work is cancelled by generation counters and CancellationTokenSources, and the reasoning is written down at each site. There are no .Result, .Wait() or .GetAwaiter().GetResult() calls anywhere — notable in a desktop app, and worth saying out loud.

Testability is the strongest single attribute. Nine of the ten services reach the app through an interface; Fakes.cs provides recording doubles, including a ThrowingDialogs whose purpose is to make a swallowed error fail the test. TempVault and TestServices make a real vault over a temp folder a two-line setup, and the headless Avalonia harness means whole windows are exercised. Rules that a UI could otherwise hide are pushed into pure functions and tested directly — VaultWorkspaceViewModel.PositionIn is the clearest example, with a comment explaining that it exists so drop semantics can be tested without a pointer.

Where the fabric is thinner. Four files read as though they were written to a different brief from the code around them — MainWindow.axaml.cs, QuickSwitcherViewModel.RefreshResults, EditorViewModel.UpdateNoteLabels and BlockRenderer.List — and the two view-layer ones are also where the fragile constructs live. JsonSettingsService is the only service with no test file of its own, and it is also the one that can stop the app from starting.

3Critical & High Findings

No Critical findings. Two High.

H1

The same write reaches disk through three entry points; only one is guarded

HighConf: High

Finding. Saving the open note happens from three places. One follows the project's error convention; two bypass it, so an ordinary, foreseeable disk failure is reported to the user as an unexpected crash.

Evidence
  • ViewModels/DialogGuard.cs:5-18 — the convention itself: "This one is for the failures a command expects; that one is for the bugs it does not. If a message arrives looking like the second, a guard is missing rather than the failure being unusual."
  • ViewModels/MainViewModel.cs:136 — Ctrl+S: _dialogs.Guard(Editor.Save). Correct.
  • ViewModels/EditorViewModel.cs:83 — the autosave timer: _saveTimer.Tick += (_, _) => { _saveTimer.Stop(); Save(); }; — bare.
  • Views/MainWindow.axaml.cs:80 — leaving the app: if (DataContext is MainViewModel vm) vm.Editor.SaveImplicitly(); — bare.
  • Contrast ViewModels/VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:114-115, where both timer ticks are wrapped: _ = _dialogs.GuardAsync(RefreshTreeAsync).
  • MdNotes.Tests/ErrorFunnelTests.cs asserts the two messages must not sound alike, but no test drives either unguarded path.
Why it matters

A note on a read-only file, a full disk, a file locked by a sync client — all ordinary — produce "Something went wrong that MdNotes did not expect… it may no longer be in a reliable state" while typing. That is alarming out of proportion to the cause, and it degrades the one diagnostic the project built on purpose: once the crash message appears for routine failures, it stops meaning "a guard is missing here". MainWindow.OnDeactivated is the worse of the two — it fires on every alt-tab, so a persistently unwritable note produces the dialog repeatedly.

Suggested direction

Route both sites through the same funnel the keyboard command uses, so that "every write the user did not explicitly ask for" has one error policy rather than two. A test in the shape of ErrorFunnelTests — make the vault write throw, drive the timer, assert the message does not contain "did not expect" — would keep it closed, and would catch the next entry point added.

H2

A corrupt settings.json stops the app from starting, before anything can report it

HighConf: High

Finding. The settings file is read with no error handling at all, from a field initialiser that runs before the crash barrier and before any window exists.

Evidence
  • Services/JsonSettingsService.cs:31-33 — _data = File.Exists(_file) ? JsonSerializer.Deserialize<Data>(File.ReadAllText(_file)) ?? new Data() : new Data(); The ?? new Data() covers only a literal null document; malformed JSON throws JsonException, and an unreadable file throws IOException.
  • AppSession.cs:17 — private readonly ISettingsService _settings = new JsonSettingsService();, a field initialiser, so it runs inside the AppSession constructor called at App.axaml.cs:44.
  • App.axaml.cs:38-42 — the crash barrier is installed at :38, but it only catches exceptions dispatched to the UI thread's loop; a throw from the constructor at :44 propagates out of StartDesktop.
  • Services/JsonSettingsService.cs:135 — the write path (File.WriteAllText) is equally unprotected, and is reached from property setters that callers treat as infallible.
  • The file is explicitly designed to be hand-edited — Models/AutoSave.cs:19 ("settings.json is hand-editable, so the value is clamped") and Models/NoteTemplate.cs:56-57 both defend against it. The read does not.
  • No JsonSettingsServiceTests.cs exists; it is the only service without one.
Why it matters

The failure mode is the worst available: the app will not start, and says nothing useful about why. A half-written file after a power cut, a sync conflict marker, or a hand edit with a trailing comma each brick the application, and the recovery (delete a JSON file in %APPDATA%) is not discoverable. Every other file this app reads — the exclusion list, the template, a note's title during a walk — already treats "will not read" as a recoverable state with a documented fallback. Settings is the exception, and it is the one read that happens before there is any way to tell the user.

Suggested direction

Treat an unreadable settings file the way IgnoreFile treats an unreadable exclusion list: fall back to defaults and carry on. Preserving the unreadable file rather than overwriting it on the next setter would matter here, since it holds the last vault path and the caret positions. Whatever the recovery, this is also the natural place for the missing test file — round-trip, defaults, and a deliberately corrupt document.

M2

Four bounded caches, four eviction policies — and the lesson learned in one was not carried to the others

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • Services/NoteContentCache.cs:17-21 documents, with measurements, that picking the LRU victim by scanning the map made a full-vault scan quadratic — "1,196 ms against 154 ms … eight times the work for two and a half times the notes" — and uses an O(1) linked list (:44,109,116).
  • Views/Markdown/Lru.cs:25 — victim chosen with MinBy over all entries.
  • Models/NotePositions.cs:42 — the same MinBy scan.
  • Views/Markdown/ImageCache.cs:36-41 — a fourth policy, generation-based rather than LRU, with a documented reason: a bitmap the visual tree still holds must not be disposed under it.
Why it matters

This is not a live performance problem — the capacities are 64, 32 and 200, so the scan is trivially bounded, and this report does not claim otherwise. The cost is comprehension: a reader asking "how does this codebase bound a cache?" gets four answers, and the one place that wrote down hard-won reasoning is the one place that reasoning was applied. If Lru's capacity is ever raised for a document-heavy vault, the same quadratic is waiting there.

Suggested direction

ImageCache solves a different problem and should stay as it is. Of the three true LRUs, Lru<TKey,TValue> is already the generic one — the question is whether the other two can share its recency mechanism, or should at least cite it. Note the trade-off the brief warns about: the three differ in value type, disposal and locking, so a forced merge could easily cost more than the duplication does.

M3

Undo skips the safety check that Replace performs

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • Services/ReplaceService.cs:58-63 — before writing, re-reads the note and calls StillMatches; a note whose text changed since the preview is skipped and named in ReplaceReport.Skipped, which the overlay reports (SearchReplaceViewModel.cs:225-228).
  • Services/ReplaceService.cs:74-78 — Restore loops the snapshot and writes unconditionally. No re-read, no comparison, no report.
  • Models/ReplaceReport.cs:3-7 documents the Skipped contract for the forward direction only.
Why it matters

Two halves of one feature enforce opposite rules and nothing says why. A reader who has just understood Replace's careful stance on concurrent edits will reasonably assume Restore shares it. Either the asymmetry is intentional — undo deliberately puts the text back regardless — in which case a sentence would settle it permanently, or it is an oversight. As written, the code does not let a reader tell those two apart.

Scope note: the data-loss consequences belong to the separate Data Safety Review. The implementation-quality point here is only the unexplained divergence between a rule and its mirror.

Suggested direction

Decide which it is and write it down at Restore. If undo should also be conservative, the mechanism it needs is ten lines away.

M4

The quick switcher rescans and re-folds the whole vault on every keystroke, unlike the other two as-you-type scans

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • ViewModels/QuickSwitcherViewModel.cs:78-81 — OnQueryChanged calls RefreshResults() directly. No timer, no thread pool, no cancellation.
  • :83-124 — each call flattens the whole tree into a fresh list (:88-89), then ranks every note (:104-111).
  • Services/NameMatch.cs:32-33 — Rank folds both the candidate and the query on every call, and QuickSwitcherViewModel.cs:105 calls it twice per note. Over 5 000 notes that is roughly ten thousand folds of the query string per keystroke, on the UI thread.
  • Compare the two features that solved this: VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:44,451-504 (250 ms debounce, thread pool, cancellation, with measurements at :469-474) and SearchReplaceViewModel.cs:21,109-156 (the same three mechanisms).
  • :94 and :110 — the Take(50) cap written once per branch.
Why it matters

Three features do "scan the vault as the user types". Two carry a written record of why that must be debounced, pooled and cancellable; the third does none of it. The tree walk itself is cached, so this is cheaper than the other two were — but it is the one still running on the UI thread, and its per-keystroke cost grows fastest with vault size. Someone adding a fourth such feature has two good examples and one counter-example, with nothing marking which is which.

Suggested direction

Two independent improvements. Hoist the folded query out of the per-candidate loop — NameMatch could take a prepared query rather than re-folding it per comparison. And bring the switcher onto the same debounce-and-cancel shape as its two siblings. The second matters more for consistency; the first matters more for cost.

M5

Two different mechanisms size the columns of one window

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • ViewModels/MainViewModel.cs:46-47 — the editor and preview columns are exposed as bound GridLength properties, consumed at Views/MainWindow.axaml:46,48.
  • Views/MainWindow.axaml.cs:54-75 — the sidebar column is mutated imperatively via Shell.ColumnDefinitions[1], driven by a hand-written PropertyChanged subscription at :28-52.
  • The index [1] silently depends on Views/MainWindow.axaml:27 — ColumnDefinitions="Auto,260,1,*". Inserting a column ahead of the sidebar compiles and then resizes the wrong one.
  • The 260 default is stated three times: the XAML above, plus MainWindow.axaml.cs:9 and :61.
Why it matters

One window, one question — how wide is this column — answered two ways, one compile-checked and one a positional index into markup. The imperative path also carries subscribe/unsubscribe bookkeeping (:32-43) that the bound path gets for free. A reader changing the shell layout has to already know the code-behind exists in order not to break it.

Suggested direction

The sidebar has a real extra requirement the other columns do not: it remembers a user-dragged width across hide and show. So this is not simply "bind it like the others". But a remembered width is state, and state belongs beside the other view state in the view model; what the view needs back from it is a GridLength, exactly as for the other two columns. Short of that, referring to the column by x:Name instead of index removes the silent breakage.

M6

Three filesystem error policies among four modules at the same boundary

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • Swallow, documented. Services/IgnoreFile.cs:5-6 — "Nothing here throws - a list that will not read means 'nothing is excluded', never a broken vault." Enforced at :32, :59, :73 through a shared IsExpected filter (:79-80).
  • Swallow, documented, narrower. Services/VaultWalk.cs:91-102 — catches IOException and UnauthorizedAccessException so a locked note falls back to its file name; explained at :82-86.
  • Propagate, undocumented. Services/FolderOrderFile.cs:34-55 — Save, Delete and Rename let every IO failure out, including from inside VaultService.MoveTo (Services/VaultService.cs:294,300-304), after the file has already moved on disk.
  • Propagate, by design. Services/NoteContentCache.cs:63,76 — documented at :56-59 as deliberately handing the caller the failure it has always had.
Why it matters

Three of the four state their policy; FolderOrderFile does not, and it is the one whose failure lands mid-mutation. A drag-and-drop where the move succeeds but the order file cannot be written raises an error dialog for an operation that visibly happened. The finally at VaultService.cs:306 correctly invalidates, so the tree ends up right — the user is simply told something failed. Anyone adding a fifth file-touching module has no rule to follow beyond reading all four.

Suggested direction

One sentence somewhere central saying when a filesystem module swallows and when it propagates — the two existing comments nearly say it already — and the same sentence on FolderOrderFile. Whether losing manual order is worth failing a completed move is a product question, but it should be a decided one.

M7

The app's only containment check keeps its preconditions in the callers, and eight of ten do not meet them

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • Services/PathScope.cs:7-9 — a case-insensitive equality plus a StartsWith(ancestor + Path.DirectorySeparatorChar) prefix test. No normalisation, no trailing-separator handling, no AltDirectorySeparatorChar.
  • Meets the preconditions (2): Services/NoteImagePath.cs:16,19-20 — resolves to a full path and trims a trailing separator from the scope; Services/NoteTemplateService.cs:44-45 — resolves to a full path only.
  • Passes paths raw (8): ViewModels/EditorViewModel.cs:49, ViewModels/VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:247,262,380,414,622, Services/VaultService.cs:272, Services/ReplaceService.cs:28.
  • The reason for normalising is a near-verbatim duplicated doc comment — NoteImagePath.cs:4-6 and NoteTemplateService.cs:35-38 — copied onto a caller that implements only half of what it describes.
  • The trailing-separator rule appears once, as a comment at NoteImagePath.cs:18: "PathScope appends the separator itself, so the scope must not already end in one."
  • The repository's own structure index records this helper as "the only mechanism keeping file operations inside the vault root."
Why it matters

Three distinct consequences, in order of how likely they are to be met. First, comprehension: the contract of the most safety-relevant function in the codebase is written in two of its callers, so a ninth caller has to find the right one to copy. Second, a vault opened at a filesystem or drive root (/, C:\) already ends in a separator, so ancestor + separator is a doubled one and containment fails — every note in such a vault is treated as a loose file and stops autosaving. Third, only the directory separator is tested, so a legal /-separated path on Windows fails containment even though NoteImagePath.cs:20 trims both.

The eight raw callers are safe today, because tree paths descend from a RootPath normalised at VaultService.cs:50 and the command-line path is normalised at VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:167. That is an invariant the code relies on and does not state.

Suggested direction

Move the preconditions inside the helper, so the two comments describing them become one implementation and the other eight callers become correct by construction rather than by luck. This is the single highest-value consolidation in the report: it is one small function, it has tests already (NoteImagePathTests), and it is the place where getting it wrong matters most.

Name validation is platform-dependent, and the suite is permanently red on Linux because of it

M9

The single-instance listener can die silently, or spin

MediumConf: High
Evidence
  • Services/SingleInstance.cs:51 — public void Listen() => _ = Task.Run(ListenLoopAsync); Fire-and-forget, with no continuation and nothing that ever observes the task.
  • :53-76 — the loop handles exactly two exception types: OperationCanceledException (:67, returns) and IOException (:71).
  • :71-74 — the IOException handler is an empty body inside the unbounded while, with only a comment.
Why it matters

Two failure shapes, both invisible. Anything outside those two types faults the task unobserved, after which every subsequent double-click in the file manager launches a second copy of MdNotes instead of a new window — a feature silently gone for the rest of the session, with no error and nothing in a log (there is no log; see L14). And a persistent IOException — a pipe name that cannot be bound, say — turns the loop into a busy spin on a background thread, since the handler neither backs off nor gives up.

Suggested direction

The empty catch needs either a bound on consecutive failures or a delay before retry, so "drop it and take the next one" cannot become "take the next one ten thousand times a second". Separately, a fire-and-forget loop that is a feature rather than a best-effort side task deserves a fault path — even just surfacing through the same crash barrier everything else uses.

MediumConf: High

M13

Three copies of one debounce interval, under a comment asserting they are one decision

MediumConf: High
Evidence

The brief asks for particular attention here, so the three findings that touch note content are called out together — with the caveat that data-loss analysis proper belongs to the Data Safety Review: H1 (an autosave failure is reported as a crash rather than as a failed save), M3 (undo does not re-check what Replace re-checks) and M8 (a note name accepted on one platform may be unrepresentable on another). Set against those, the protections that are genuinely well built deserve equal mention: StillMatches guards the forward replace, NoteContentCache.Store exists so the app never trusts a timestamp for text it wrote itself, FlushUnsavedAsync funnels every path that moves off a dirty note, and MoveTo and Rename invalidate in a finally so a mid-sequence failure cannot leave a stale tree.

9AI-Assisted Development Smells

Scope of this section. It reports observable maintainability consequences only. Nothing here claims any code was AI-generated, and nothing here could establish that. The patterns below are the ones the brief asks about; they occur in hand-written codebases too, and several of them here are clearly the residue of ordinary iterative development — the codebase's own comments describe the refactors that produced them.

Most items in the catalogue simply are not present. There are no wrappers layered over wrappers, no generic infrastructure built for a single hypothetical consumer, no unreachable compatibility shims, no speculative configuration surface, and no over-application of interfaces — the eight service interfaces all exist because a test substitutes them, which is a demonstrated need rather than a guessed one. What follows is what was actually observed.

Observed

Comments that no longer match the implementation — the strongest signal here. Four, all of the same kind: a comment asserting single-sourcing that the code no longer provides. Services/VaultWalk.cs:17-18 declares "the one place that decides" above the first of four ".md" declarations (M1). Services/VaultService.cs:129-133 declares "the one place that answers it" above one of three title fallbacks (L11). ViewModels/VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:42-43 describes three debounce literals as one decision (M13). And the precondition comment at Services/NoteImagePath.cs:4-6 was copied near-verbatim onto Services/NoteTemplateService.cs:35-38, a caller that implements only half of what it describes (M7). In a codebase where comments carry this much weight, a comment that has quietly become false is more expensive than elsewhere.

Multiple representations of one concept. Three LRU eviction strategies (M2); two rules for "does this name match" (M12); three tree-flatten-with-label routines (M11); two mechanisms for sizing a grid column in one window (M5). Each was locally reasonable where it was written. The collective effect is that several common questions have more than one answer in this repository.

Near-duplicate helpers. Two Count pluralisers, two "append the extension" implementations against two different constants, the same exception filter as a named predicate and inline, the same relative-key expression written twice sixty lines apart (L3, M1, M10).

A forwarding layer with no behaviour. ViewModels/MainViewModel.cs:119-125 — three methods that forward verbatim, each carrying a copy of the doc comment it forwards to (L4).

One abstraction with a single real consumer. The generic Lru<TKey,TValue> (Views/Markdown/Lru.cs) is used only by DiagramCache, twice — and meanwhile the two other bounded maps in the codebase each rolled their own. A generic written for one consumer while its natural second and third consumers went their own way is the shape worth noticing, more than the generic itself.

Premature configurability. Mild and mostly defensible. DiagramCache(renderer, pictureCapacity = 64, bitmapCapacity = 32), NoteContentCache(budgetChars = 32_000_000) and NotePositions(capacity = 200) all take parameters that production never varies — but each is used by tests to force eviction, which is a real consumer. NoteTemplateService.ContentFor(folderPath, name) (Services/NoteTemplateService.cs:14) is the honest case: folderPath is unused, and the class comment says so and says what it is for. That is a documented hook, not a hidden one.

Locally reasonable additions that add up. The clearest instance is the settings pipeline (CA1). Each of its four propagation routes was a sensible answer to the setting in front of its author — the theme is app-wide, so broadcast it; note titles need the tree rebuilt, so call the vault; the gitignore switch already had a service, so use it; auto-save affects the editor, so hand the editor a callback. Four good local decisions, and the result is that the fifth setting has no obvious right answer and the fourth one is already subtly wrong across windows.

Not observed, and worth recording as such

  • No excessive defensive checks. Null handling is proportionate; the codebase uses ?? throw with a real message where null means a bug, and pattern matching where it does not.
  • No repeated fallback behaviour. Fallbacks exist (theme colours, note titles, display names) and each has a stated reason.
  • No obsolete compatibility code. Nothing in the tree is unreachable, and there are no version checks or migration paths for shapes that no longer exist.
  • No unnecessary conversion chains. Data crosses each layer once. The one place that converts and converts back — milliseconds to seconds in the settings panel (ViewModels/SettingsViewModel.cs:141,144) — has a one-line comment explaining that nobody thinks in milliseconds.
  • No needless copying of significant data. The snapshot hands the same array instance to every caller and says so (Services/VaultWalk.cs:78); the content cache exists specifically to stop re-reading. The one repeated transformation is diacritic folding over whole notes per search (Services/SearchService.cs:28), and Fold short-circuits to return the original string when nothing folds.

10Language / Framework Usage

C# and Avalonia are used well, and mostly idiomatically for .NET 10. This section reports only the cases where usage materially affects clarity, correctness, maintainability, performance or testability — not style.

Used well

  • Modern C# where it earns its place. Primary constructors on services, collection expressions, list patterns (name.IndexOfAny(['/', '\\'])), property patterns (is { IsFolder: true } folder), [GeneratedRegex] for the two hot regexes, and string.Create with a static lambda in DiacriticFolder to fold without an intermediate allocation.
  • System.Threading.Lock (VaultService.cs:13, NoteContentCache.cs:39) rather than lock (object) — the .NET 9+ idiom.
  • The MVVM toolkit used as intended. [ObservableProperty] with partial properties, [NotifyPropertyChangedFor] for derived values, [RelayCommand(CanExecute = ...)], and generated On…Changed partials for side effects. No hand-written INotifyPropertyChanged plumbing anywhere in the view models.
  • Cancellation done properly. Tokens threaded into the service, checked per note with a comment explaining the granularity choice, OperationCanceledException caught at exactly the right boundary, and a second post-await check for the race the exception cannot catch (VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:494-496).
  • Avalonia used natively rather than fought. StyledProperty with class handlers, AttachedProperty for InitialFocus, a templated ContentControl for the overlay, compiled bindings on by default (MdNotes.csproj:10), and rebuilds coalesced at DispatcherPriority.Render.
  • No ConfigureAwait anywhere — correct for a UI framework where continuations must resume on the UI thread, and consistently so.

Where it costs something

  • Nullable warnings are only warnings. Nullable is enabled (MdNotes.csproj:7), but there is no TreatWarningsAsErrors, no WarningsAsErrors=Nullable, no .editorconfig and no Directory.Build.props. The discipline visible in the code is entirely the authors' — the build does not enforce it. Given how little nullable debt exists (nine ! in 6 500 lines), turning the warnings into errors would cost almost nothing now and would keep it that way.
  • Hand-rolled properties where the store is a service. SettingsViewModel's four properties cannot use [ObservableProperty] because the value lives in ISettingsService, so the guard-write-notify shape is repeated by hand (CA1). Not misuse — the generator does not cover this case — but it is where the settings boilerplate comes from.
  • Custom code standing in for framework behaviour, all justified. LinkHitTesting maps clicks to character ranges because Avalonia has no clickable inline; VaultWalk is hand-rolled because SearchOption.AllDirectories cannot be stopped from descending; NoteTitle scans by hand rather than adding a YAML dependency; SvgCssInliner exists because Svg.Skia has no CSS. Each carries its reason in a comment. This is the right instinct and the right documentation of it.
  • One framework-adjacent fragility. Views/Shell/Sidebar.axaml.cs:144,149 reaches into the Fluent theme's template by part name. Already flagged in-code at :133-139, with SidebarDragTests as the tripwire — the right handling of an unavoidable dependency.

11Positive Findings

These are the implementation choices that should survive any future refactor. Several are listed with the same specificity as the problems, because "keep doing this" is only actionable if it names the thing.

P1

Comments that record reasoning, not restatement

The single best attribute of this codebase. Comments say why a decision was made, what was tried before, and what it cost — often with numbers. Services/NoteContentCache.cs:3-29 explains the budget with measured timings and names the failure mode past it. Services/AppServices.cs:8-12 records the fourteen-site edit it eliminated. Services/VaultWalk.cs:5-13 says which three walks it replaced and what they disagreed about. ViewModels/TreeNodeViewModel.cs:91-98 explains why this comparison is deliberately stricter than the one two files away. Views/Shell/ModalOverlay.cs:12-16 names the bug that three copies of the chrome caused. A reader can reconstruct the reasoning behind almost any non-obvious line without asking anyone.

This is also why M13, M1 and L11 matter more here than they would elsewhere: in a codebase this comment-driven, a comment that has become false is a trap rather than a nuisance.

P2

The two-tier error funnel, and the tests that hold it in place

ViewModels/DialogGuard.cs handles failures a command foresees and shows the message they were thrown with; App.axaml.cs:38-42 catches what escapes and says the failure was unexpected. The design is good on its own, but what makes it durable is MdNotes.Tests/ErrorFunnelTests.cs, which asserts that the two do not sound alike — pinning the property the design exists for rather than its implementation. That the distinction is testable is why H1 could be stated as a defect rather than as an opinion.

P3

Rules pulled out of the UI so they can be tested without one

VaultWorkspaceViewModel.PositionIn (:363-372) is the model: the sidebar measures where in a row the pointer is, and this static function says what the user meant by it. The comment states the intent exactly — "This is a product rule, not a geometry detail… so it can be exercised without a pointer-driven UI test." The same instinct produced ResolveDrop, NameMatch, NoteTitle, VaultIgnore, NoteTemplate and MatchFinder — all pure, all directly tested.

P4

Test infrastructure that makes the right test easy to write

TempVault gives a real VaultService over a temp folder and seeds it behind the service's back so no test waits out a file watcher. TestServices.For builds the ten-member service set in one line. Fakes.cs provides recording doubles that count what happened (PositionSaves, Saves) rather than merely allowing it — and ThrowingDialogs inverts the usual default so that a test whose point is "nothing broke" fails loudly if anything was swallowed. The headless Avalonia harness means whole windows, drag targeting and focus behaviour are all covered. Fifty-one test files against seventy-nine production files is a ratio most projects claim and few have.

P5

Concurrency reasoning written down at every site

Three vault-wide scans run off the UI thread, each with a measured justification in the comment above it. Cancellation uses both a token and a post-await check, with the race the second one covers explained at VaultWorkspaceViewModel.cs:494-496. The tree walk uses a generation counter so a slow walk finishing late drops its answer rather than reinstating an old vault (:51-56). NoteContentCache holds its lock around the map but explicitly not around the disk read, and says why (:34-38). VaultService.CurrentSnapshot documents the one field it reads unlocked and names what compensates. No .Result, no .Wait(), no deadlock surface.

P6

Single-sourced rules where it counted most

One disk walk answering the tree, the notes and the folders. One find shared by the replace preview and the replace itself, so what is written is what was shown. One name-matching rule behind both wikilinks and the switcher. One overlay chrome behind all three overlays. One service record behind every window. Each of these removed a class of bug rather than a class of typing, and each is documented as having done so. M1, M12 and M11 are all "the same instinct, one level up" — which is the least alarming kind of finding to have.

P7

Contained side effects and honest state transitions

EditorViewModel.SetContent (:222-243) is representative: stop the timers, remember the outgoing caret, set path and text under a loading flag, restore the incoming caret — with a comment explaining why the order matters. SaveImplicitly is the single gate for every write the user did not ask for, and FlushUnsavedAsync is the single gate for every path that moves off a dirty note; both are named for what they mean rather than what they do. Mutation is confined to the service layer, and every mutation ends at one Invalidate().

14Overall Maintainability Assessment

Ask the question the brief sets: does this actually make the repository harder to understand, change, test or debug? For most of MdNotes the answer is no, and that should be the first conclusion rather than a hedge at the end of a list.

Understanding it is easy. The layering is legible, the names say what things are, and the comments carry the reasoning. A reader landing in an unfamiliar file learns not just what the code does but what it replaced and why. That is rare, and it is the property most worth protecting.

Testing it is easy, and demonstrably so. Rules are extracted into pure functions on purpose, with the purpose stated. Services sit behind interfaces because tests substitute them, and the fakes are built to make silence fail. Whole windows run headless. The 51 test files are not a formality.

Changing it is mostly easy, with one real exception. Adding a service costs one line because the project fixed that. Adding an overlay is cheap because the project fixed that too. Adding a command is tolerable. Adding a setting is the outlier — eight edits and a choice between four undocumented propagation routes, one of which has already been chosen wrong in a way that shows up only with two windows open. That is the one place the codebase has not yet applied its own lesson.

Debugging it is the weakest axis. Not because the code is opaque, but because nothing is recorded. Twenty-two catch blocks, none of which log; two that say nothing at all. When a user reports that exclusions stopped applying or that a second launch opened a second copy of the app, there is no evidence to ask for. That is a defensible trade for a local, private notes app — it is listed as a trade-off rather than a defect — but it is the reason several findings here are hard to confirm from the outside.

The pattern worth taking away

Four separate findings share one shape: a comment asserts single-sourcing that the code no longer provides. "The one place that decides" above the first of four ".md" declarations. "The one place that answers it" above one of three title fallbacks. "The interval the other two timers use" above three unlinked literals. A precondition explained in one caller and honoured by no other. In a codebase where comments are the primary way knowledge is transferred, these are not cosmetic. They are the mechanism by which a good practice — writing down the reasoning — decays into a misleading one, because the reader who trusts the comment stops checking. Every one of them is a sentence or a constant away from being true again.

The second pattern is milder: four caches, three error policies, two column mechanisms, two name-matching rules, four settings routes. None is wrong. Each was a reasonable local answer. Collectively they mean several ordinary questions have more than one answer here, and a new contributor has to read all of them to know which to copy.

Direction

Roughly a day of work covers the top of §12: two guards, one try/catch, one constant, one helper tightened, and four comments made true. That closes both High findings, the most safety-relevant Medium, and the decay pattern above. The settings pipeline is a separate, larger decision and is the only item here that warrants planning rather than just doing.

Nothing in this review argues for restructuring. The architecture is not the problem, the file sizes are not the problem, and the abstractions that exist have earned their place. What is worth defending is the comment culture and the test seams — they are why this review could be specific, and they are what the findings above are mostly in service of keeping accurate.
